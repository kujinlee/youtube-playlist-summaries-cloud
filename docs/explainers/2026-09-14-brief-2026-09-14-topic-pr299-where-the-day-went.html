PR #299 — where the day actually went

Seventeen review rounds, about seven hours of machine time, and roughly three of those hours bought nothing. This is the accounting, and the reason the round counter kept moving.

The short answer

The reviews were not the expensive part. All seven Codex rounds together cost about 1 hour 20 minutes. The mutation gate cost 3 hours 45 minutes — nearly three times as much — and a single hung process cost 1 hour 53 minutes on its own.

The rounds kept coming because the stopping condition was misread. The coordinator tracked severity, saw it falling, and predicted round 15 would close the loop. Severity was never the variable. What each round was pointed at was: rounds aimed at the instrument found instrument defects, and every single round aimed at the deliverable found a fail-open in it — four times out of four, including the last one before the human called a halt.

About three of the seven hours were pure waste, and they were not review time. They were 1h53m watching a process that had already finished its work, and ~1h15m re-running a 25-minute mutation gate that had correctly refused to answer.

The Claude halves ran as concurrent subagents overlapping the Codex rounds, so they add little wall-clock and are not summed. The fix / adjudicate / file cycles between rounds are real time and were never separately measured — they are the honest gap in this accounting.

The surprise, stated plainly

The intuition going in is that a seventeen-round review is slow because of the reviewing. It was not. Verifying each fix cost 2.8× what finding the defects cost. Every accepted finding triggered a ~25-minute mutation run, and a round that produced one High therefore cost roughly fifteen minutes of review and twenty-five of proof.

Why there were seventeen rounds at all

Ten of them were already spent before the interesting part began, and for a structural reason rather than a bad one.

The branch was built by a worker fork, and a worker fork cannot spawn a second reviewer. This project's gate requires two independent halves — Codex and Claude. So rounds 1 through 10 ran Codex-only, and each one wrote the shortfall down in its own document:

REVIEW GAP: claude

Ten times. That is the process working — the gap was declared, not hidden — but it also means the branch reached round 10 with one entire reviewer's worth of debt outstanding. When the owed half finally ran as round 11, it was discharged as one whole-branch review against the shipping tree rather than ten retroactive ones, because re-reviewing superseded trees would violate the very rule the branch was shipping.

And the first thing the owed half found was a working evasion of the rule. Round 11's Blocking: a reverted dirty overlay was compared by nobody. Under the documented practice — hold your fixes uncommitted so the reviewer sees what merges — the reviewer's copy is not the committed one. Drop that fix and the path stops counting as “changed since the round” and quietly leaves the comparison set. Reviewed as GOOD, merged as BAD, exit 0 under the message “the final tree was reviewed by…”

Running the owed half five more times found three more ways past. That is rounds 11 through 16. Round 17 was a verification pass, not a review.

The four fail-opens, and what they had in common

All four were in scripts/check-review-recorded.py — the gate the branch exists to ship. Each one was sitting inside a sentence an earlier round had already declared clean.

r14git diff --name-only with rename detection on by default — a guarded file moved to a prose path simply vanished, and the gate reported “no guarded path changed” over a deleted code file.
r15docs/ is not prose in this repository. It holds two of the fifteen schema gates. Both halves of the gate went silent over executable CI gate code.

r15 is the one that shows the pattern

The classifier held PROSE_DIRS = ("docs/",) — everything under docs/ is documentation, therefore harmless, therefore no review round owed. Two of the project's fifteen schema gates live there. Verified on the current tree, not quoted from the review:

Mode 755. Run by check-schema-gates.sh. Listed as path-filter triggers by .github/workflows/schema-gates.yml — the workflow itself declares them gate subjects. A branch that weakened a mutation in mutate-schema.py could have merged with no review round and no declaration.

⭐ And round 11 had explicitly checked this and said it was fine. The r11 Claude half states, in writing, that it “searched docs/ for executable or consumed artefacts that would be wrongly exempt: everything found is *.md and docs/reviews/verdicts/*.json.” Two exist. The branch's own first review wrote the sentence that hid the defect for four more rounds — and round 15 found it only by re-deriving that claim instead of inheriting it. The identical move had found r14's rename fail-open one round earlier.

r16 is the one that should be uncomfortable

Round 15 did the right thing. Having found that the exemption list could drift from what CI actually treats as gate code, it built a guard against exactly that: prose_exceptions_cover(globs), pure, taking the workflow's globs as an argument.

Round 16 found that the guard's own test fed it a hand-typed copy of the workflow's globs. There were three copies in play, and it was comparing the wrong two:

#1 the workflow — the original → #2 CODE_UNDER_PROSE → #3 the test case · compared: #3 vs #2

Neither side of the comparison was the original. Add a new gate directory to CI and the anti-drift guard would report everything fine. And main never called it at all. Verified on the merged tree — both halves of that are now fixed:

$ grep -n "prose_exceptions_cover" scripts/check-review-recorded.py
213:  def prose_exceptions_cover(workflow_globs: list[str]) -> list[str]:
899:      _uncovered = prose_exceptions_cover(_globs)          <- main calls it now
1012:     prose_exceptions_cover(workflow_docs_globs(_WF or "")), [])  <- reads the REAL file
The shape, in one sentence. The mechanism built to prevent the defect rebuilt the defect. Round 15's fix was a second copy of a path set, and its falsifier was a third copy — so the guard was structurally incapable of noticing the thing it was built to notice.

The 1 h 53 m, which was not review time

The single largest block of wasted wall-clock on this branch was a waiter watching the wrong thing.

TimeWhat was trueWhat the session reported
12:50Codex wrote the r16 review (2,923 chars) and its verdict. Its work was done.“still running”
13:xxBoth files on disk, unchanged.“still running”
14:44Process finally killed by hand.“still running”

The waiter looped on pgrep -f "r16-prompt" — it polled the process. The deliverable was a file, and the file had existed for nearly two hours. Three status reports said “still running” over a completed review.

⛔ The rule against this already existed, and is loaded into every session. docs/plugins.md says: “never passively wait on a background review … within ~2–3 minutes, read the actual Codex task output file … treat as a hang → fall back.” That file is imported by CLAUDE.md. It was broken three times in one session — reconstructed from recall at the moment of use rather than read. This is the same shape as the selection-card rule that went 1-for-3 while written down in three places.

The falsifier nobody ran was one stat: does the output file exist while the waiter still says running? It would have caught this on the first status report.

Filed as backlog #115 (the waiter) and #116 (the hang itself — a wrapper that stays alive after succeeding, cause undiagnosed and recorded as such, because this branch had already spent a day paying for confident sentences about causes nobody measured).

The three refusals that cost ~1 h 15 m and were worth every minute

The mutation gate ran nine times. Three came back refusing to answer. Each refusal cost a ~25-minute run and each one was correct.

RunEntriesVerdictCause
mutate4626NOT MEASURED — 625 of 626r15's fix rewrote second_question's body; anchors bind by text
mutate7629CANNOT RUN — control red 138/139a new case read schema-gates.yml, which HARNESS_TREE did not stage
mutate8629NOT MEASURED — 628 of 629a fix rewrote another body and renamed a case an entry named
mutate9629629 killed · 629 attributed · 0 survivors—
Why a refusal beats the pass it eventually gave. 625 of 626 is exactly the shape that reads as success if you only look at the kill count. The harness refused rather than reporting 625 kills, because one declared mutation had silently stopped applying — and an unapplied mutation's “caught” verdict is meaningless. A green tally would have hidden it.

The mechanism: an entry binds to its target twice

A mutation entry names its target by anchor text (the code it patches) and by case name (the test it expects to go red). A fix that rewrites a function body breaks the first. A fix that renames a test breaks the second.

A pre-flight that checks one binding passes exactly when the other breaks. That is the finding, and it is filed as backlog #115's companion shape. Two 25-minute runs died to teach it.

A demonstration, produced while writing this page

The branch's headline instruction is re-derive, do not inherit, and its most-repeated failure is a weaker stand-in disagreeing with its subject. Both reproduced in two commands while checking the numbers on this page.

To confirm the mutation total, a regex was written to read EXPECTED_MUTATIONS out of check-plan-code.py:

regex stand-in  ->  files: 59   sum: 3067
                    (confident, specific, and wrong by 5x — it swept up
                     numbers from elsewhere in the file)

Importing the module and reading its own constant instead:

import importlib.util, sys
spec = importlib.util.spec_from_file_location("cpc", "scripts/check-plan-code.py")
m = importlib.util.module_from_spec(spec)
sys.modules["cpc"] = m            # dataclasses needs the module registered
spec.loader.exec_module(m)

files: 44   sum: 629
scripts/check-review-recorded.py -> 43
scripts/codex-review.py          -> 12

Which matches the PR body exactly. The repo's own rule — import the subject's function, never re-derive it — was written because of defects like the one above, and it took about ninety seconds to reproduce it by ignoring the rule.

The largest single saving is the cheapest fix on the list, and the rule for it was already written down.

Did the seventeen rounds earn their cost?

On the evidence: yes, and the last useful round was the second-to-last one.

  • Nothing shipped broken. Four fail-opens in a gate whose entire purpose is to refuse unreviewed code — every one found, every one fixed, none merged.
  • The rounds never went quiet. r16 — the final review round, run under a human pre-commitment to merge afterwards regardless — still returned a High. There is no round at which stopping would have been obviously safe.
  • But the stopping rule had to come from outside. The human made the call that round 16 was the last, with the trade-off stated in full: on this branch a fix generated the next round's defect five times out of six. A convergence rule based on “no findings” would still be running.

The three sentences worth keeping

  1. Re-derive, do not inherit. Every round that re-checked a previous “we looked, it's clean” found something. Two of the four fail-opens were sitting inside such a sentence, one of them written by this branch's own first review.
  2. Track what a round is aimed at, not what severity it returned. Severity said converging; aim said zero-for-four on the deliverable. Aim was right.
  3. Wait on the artifact, never on the process. One stat was the difference between a two-minute check and 1 h 53 m.
