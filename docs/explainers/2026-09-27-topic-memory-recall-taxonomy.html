COMPRESSION OPTIMISES THE WRONG AXIS — THIS IS THE ROOT CAUSE

With 57 characters you keep what is most informative: the surprising detail, the cost. Recognition needs what is most matchable: the situation. Those are different, and the pressure systematically evicts the matchable half — because “when indexing a list by position” reads as generic and obvious, while “cells[-2] closed 2 open items” reads as sharp and memorable.

The 84% trigger-blindness is therefore not carelessness. It is an optimisation aimed at the wrong target, which is why more discipline would not have fixed it and a structural field will.

Cause 2 — the curse of knowledge. The hook is written by whoever just had the failure, when cells[-2] is maximally salient. The author cannot simulate the future reader's state of not-knowing. That 29% lean on a backticked token is the tell: they bet on a distinctive string the author can no longer stop seeing.

Cause 3 — no feedback loop, which is why hooks never improve. Nothing ever reports “your hook did not fire.” The only evidence of a miss is a defect weeks later that nobody attributes to the hook. So hooks are written once, under the curse of knowledge, compressed to 57 characters, and never corrected.

Which is why A1 is structural rather than disciplinary — a named FIRES-WHEN: field defeats all three: compression cannot silently evict a named field (a missing one is visibly missing); it forces the author to state what salience hides; and once a matcher exists, a miss becomes observable for the first time.

7The design, in three parts

Part A — mechanism is primary, because it is the only remedy with prior wins

A mechanism does not need recall. That is not an argument, it is the observed record: of the eight repairs attempted in the session that produced #191, exactly one works without the author remembering anything — a guard. The other seven are notes competing with 9,979 lines. Two earlier mechanism wins are on record: the selection-card hook, and PR #354's escape cross-check.

The promotion rule — the crisp, decidable part of this design

A memory entry graduates to a hook when its trigger is exactly decidable from the text of a tool call.

Your own candidate is the worked example: refuse git commit -m whose argument contains a backtick, a double quote, or a newline. That is exactly decidable — no judgement, no false positives, and it fires at the moment of the mistake rather than hoping to be remembered before it. It generalises to the rule behind it: never pass prose as a shell argument — --body-file, --prompt-file, git commit -F.

Entries whose trigger needs judgement cannot be promoted. “Measure the population the code actually sees” has no tool-call signature. Those fall to Part B, and Part B is explicitly the weaker half — that is the honest structure of this design, not a hedge.

Part B — for the residue: re-key the index by trigger, and keep single ownership

The synthesis that keeps #177 intact — because #191 is explicit that duplication is the vulnerability and #177's one owner per fact is the first-order remedy:

One owner per fact, N pointers by trigger

The entry file stays subject-keyed and single-owned, exactly as now. The index gains a trigger-keyed view whose rows are pointers, not content.

Pointers may safely appear under several triggers because they carry no facts — there is nothing in them to drift. This is the process-rationale.md pointer-row pattern, which #191 names as the one read-trigger mechanism measured to work here.

Candidate top-level triggers are operations — observable from what I am about to do, not from what I would have to realise:

  • about to open a PR · about to commit
  • about to claim something is measured · about to trust a green check
  • about to write a guard or a mutation · about to spawn agents
  • about to close a message to the user · about to build something that may already exist

Part C — the falsifier, sharper than #191's

#191's falsifier is “a class already present in memory recurs, and the postmortem shows the entry existed and was not found.” After re-keying that is too weak, because a trigger-keyed index changes what found means. The sharper form:

What must be observed for Part B to be judged wrong

A class recurs · the entry existed under the correct trigger · and it still was not read. → keying is not the constraint, and mechanism is the only remaining answer.

The baseline this has to beat is doing nothing — not “doing something”. If Part A alone removes the classes that actually cost money, Part B is ceremony, and its own falsifier is what would show that.

8Where I disagree with your framing

You suggested the taxonomy's top level may be principles rather than topics, because a principle is itself a search-area reducer. My measurements push toward operations instead, and I want to put the disagreement in front of you rather than quietly resolve it.

The case for operations

An operation is observable. I am literally about to run gh pr create. A principle still requires me to recognise that it applies — and recognition is the exact step that failed in every one of the six sampled classes.

The case for principles — steelmanned

A principle fires across many operations. “Measure the population the code actually sees” applies to a guard, a CI check, a backlog row and a review. Keying it to operations duplicates it into every branch — and duplication is #191's stated vulnerability, the very thing #177 was built to remove.

My proposed resolution, which is itself contestable: the duplication a principle would suffer under operation-keying is duplication of pointers, and a pointer carries no fact, so there is nothing to drift. The principle keeps exactly one owner — its entry file — and appears as a pointer under each operation where it fires. If you think that understates the cost of a pointer going stale when its owner is renamed, that is the strongest counter-argument and I would rather hear it now than after building.

Family 1 — make the STORED item carry its own situation (the one with a measured effect size)

Anthropic's Contextual Retrieval prepends a short explanatory context to each chunk before it is indexed, so the chunk carries the circumstance it belongs to rather than floating free. Published figures, on top-20 retrieval failure rate:

⭐ This is the single most relevant result in the survey, because it is §7's Part B under another name. The fix is not a better search over the same corpus — it is rewriting what is stored so that it carries the situation it applies to. That is exactly “re-key the index by trigger”, and it now has an industry name and a number attached.

Family 2 — hybrid retrieval and reranking

Lexical (BM25) catches exact identifiers; embeddings catch paraphrase; a reranker does deeper analysis on the small candidate set the first two produce. Relevant here because §3 measured that a bare lexical probe returns 34 of 141 files for anchor — which is precisely the imprecision a reranking stage exists to fix. ⚠ But it presumes a retrieval step, and §10 measured that there is none.

Family 3 — agent-memory architectures, and the tiering that matters

Letta/MemGPT uses an explicitly OS-inspired split: core memory always in context (RAM), archival memory in an external searchable store (disk), recall memory for history — and the agent calls tools to search the lower tiers. Mem0 does passive extraction plus semantic search; Zep/Graphiti adds a bi-temporal knowledge graph.

THE TIERING IS THE FINDING, NOT THE VENDOR

MEMORY.md is currently 100% core memory. All 133 rows, always resident, no archival tier and no tool to search one. Every one of these systems concluded independently that this shape stops working past a certain size — which is the same conclusion §1 reached by measurement, arrived at by people who had to ship it.

⚠ Treat their benchmark numbers with suspicion. Zep reports 71.2% on LongMemEval against Mem0's 49%; Mem0 separately claims 94.4 on the same benchmark. Vendor-published figures that disagree this violently are marketing until independently replicated, and I am not going to launder them by quoting only one.

Family 4 — do not retrieve at all: enforce at the point of action

The most widely adopted answer in ordinary software is not a memory system. Danger.js exists to automate review chores and its tagline is literally the problem statement — “Stop saying ‘you forgot to…’”. Twitch pairs CODEOWNERS with lint rules to steer a monolith: ownership routes the change, lint snapshots stop the pattern spreading. Policy-as-code routes risky operations to a human at the moment they are attempted.

The argument, in the sharpest form the search produced: a constraint that exists only in institutional memory degrades over time, while a lint rule enforces the constraint forever.

factory.ai makes the agent-specific version of this case — that linters beat markdown instruction files for directing agents, naming three failure modes of prose guidance: ambiguity (the agent believes it complied), no enforcement (advice does not fail a build, so drift accumulates quietly), and limited scope (prose cannot verify cross-file boundaries). ⛔ It offers no measurements of any kind — no benchmark, no case study, no numbers. It is an argument, and a good one, but it is not evidence, and this page is not going to cite it as though it were.

Family 5 — the cognitive science, which names what you described

Your framing has a formal name and a fifty-year literature: Tulving and Thomson's encoding specificity principle — retrieval succeeds to the extent that the cues present at recall overlap the cues present at encoding. Context encoded alongside a memory is what later evokes it.

⭐ That is this page's whole finding, stated by psychologists in 1973. The index encodes by SUBJECT (“what this lesson is about”). Retrieval is attempted in a SITUATION (“I am about to open a PR”). The cues do not overlap, so the trace is not evoked — and §3 measured the non-overlap directly: 1 of 133 rows opens by naming a situation.

  • Your breadcrumbs raise the SECOND — enrich the moment of retrieval.
  • Family 5 is the law saying either works.

Frequency, derived from the plans on disk rather than estimated: 45 plan files, 195 steps, 3.5 plans/day ≈ 15 intent-statements a day, against the refuted hook's ~275 tool-call invocations per session. A model call costs 16.1s. ⭐⭐ And the reframe: as the corpus grows, genuine negatives become scarce

At 141 entries adding a not-null column matches nothing. At 300 it matches something apt. At 1000 covering all of engineering, nearly every moment legitimately matches something. So the precision question changes character with scale — from does it fire wrongly to is this correct match worth interrupting for. That is a relevance question, not a correctness one, and a similarity shortlist cannot answer it, because ranking by importance is not ranking by similarity. It is a third mechanism, and it is arguably what #191 was about all along.

⛔ Do not set the embedding trigger on a corpus size. Size was measured and does not bind at 1000. Build the next stage when relevance becomes the binding problem — when correct-but-not-worth-saying matches outnumber the useful ones.
